author: "Ethan Perez-Rivera"
abstract: "TSEC/KW-26 was an early generation electronic crypto-machine that provided confidentiality for American teletypewriter communications starting in 1957. Developed by the National Security Agency and manufactured by the Burroughs Corporation, the KW-26 took approximately five years to go from design to production. Over the course of more than twenty-five years, about 14 thousand or 15 thousand devices worked for NSA, U.S. armed forces, DCA, State Department, and CIA. This paper shows that although the KW-26 provided good level of confidentiality and availability, it did not provide any integrity at all. One of the limitations is described – vulnerability of XOR-based encryption to modification. The author suggests a message authentication code (MAC) for protection of integrity."

# Introduction

Each day, one three cent punched card did the work of approximately $20 worth of one-time key tape [2]. This trade was enabled by the TSEC/KW-26, which is a first generation, mass produced electronic encryption machine that offered on-line, synchronous security for teletypewriter record traffic at all classification levels [1]. The system was internally designed by the National Security Agency (NSA), manufactured by the Burroughs Corporation, and first shipped in 1957 [1], [2]. Approximately 14,000 to 15,000 units were ultimately deployed, and the KW-26 remained in use for more than 25 years before decommissioning began in the mid-1980s [1], [2]. However, the KW-26's significance extended well beyond the above numbers. The KW-26 superseded the SIGTOT and most of the one-time tape machines on high-level teletypewriter circuits, and Boak says that more than any other system, the KW-26 proved to the doubtful military cryptographers that the young NSA should be the nation's cryptographic authority [2]. This paper will study the development history, deployment and its impact on confidentiality, integrity, and availability (CIA), general characteristics, users, successors, retirement, and a security limitation together with a potential solution.

# History of Development

By the mid-twentieth century, the teletypewriter became the standard vehicle for record traffic; however, the teletypewriter itself did not have any security built into it, and, hence, sensitive messages required their own separate encryption system [1]. One of the driving forces behind the development of the KW-26 was the cost consideration. One time tape from Vernam's invention was unbreakable provided that every key was used just once. However, due to high costs of producing, delivering, and finally destroying large quantities of the key tape, this technique was utilized only for the most sensitive communications [1]. There was also an operational need. Rotor machines such as SIGABA improved the key management process; however, they were very complicated and used off-line. Hence, encryption was a time-consuming process carried out separately. The Army Security Agency (ASA) also wanted a high-speed on-line substitute for the British 5-UCO one-time-tape technique [1]. Lastly, the motivation was strategic. During World War II, Allied breaks of Enigma and Purple stimulated the creation of a high quality in-house cryptographic capability in the Department of Defense, which resulted in establishing the Armed Forces Security Agency in 1949 and NSA in 1952 [1].

NSA was both the sponsor and the designer. The Record and Data division, under Howard Barlow, conducted the internal research, and Joe Hannan was the head of the group that developed the first breadboard model [1]. Barlow's aim was a cheap, inconspicuous add-on to existing communications equipment that would never be a bottleneck and whose cryptographical power would correspond to how long the traffic needed protection [1]. The first goal is indicative of a fourth motive, infrastructure, which required the KW-26 to fit into the existing communication networks rather than require new ones. Achieving such goals required abandoning the old ways in three areas: vacuum tubes and magnetic cores were used in place of relays and wired rotors, keystream generator took the place of one-time tape, and punched cards replaced key lists [1]. Nevertheless, the system retained two well-tried elements of the 5-UCO: Vernam's modulo-2 (XOR) combiner and traffic flow security, which hid any indication of whether real messages were being communicated or not [1].

NSA developed a breadboard using vacuum tubes in 1952 and gave a sole-source development contract to Burroughs Corporation in 1953 where Lyle Thompson conducted research on magnetic cores for the design [1]. The breadboard successfully completed a test on a looped overseas circuit in 1954 and Burroughs Corporation delivered its first model in 1955 [1]. Final engineering models were delivered to the customers in 1957 for testing; after a bad batch of ceramic capacitors was discovered and replaced, NSA ordered its first production run of 1,500 units [1]. Development from breadboard to production took roughly five years. Boak's comment that the KW-26 went from conception to hardware in roughly 18 months [2] should be seen as referring to the core design work.

# History of Deployment and Impact

## Deployment

KW-26 came into use in 1957 with the first NSA production order of 1,500 KW-26 units delivered for CRITICOMM and other signals intelligence (SIGINT) applications [1]. CRITICOMM was the network designated by the Joint Chiefs of Staff to provide the communications channels necessary for national SIGINT [1]. It became the machine's most intensive consumer, and in March 1973 there were 336 KW-26s operating simultaneously at NSA headquarters at Fort Meade, where CRITICOMM terminated [2]. Starting from the early 1960s, additional purchases increased total production above 14,000 units and extended its use throughout the government [1]. Another large user was the Defense Communications Agency (DCA). In its early years, DCA depended upon the KW-26 as the primary encryptor of the AUTODIN trunks forming the defense communications network [1]. The Navy used it on shore stations and ships, the Army and Air Force on tactical point-to-point channels, and the State Department and CIA on their respective point-to-point networks [1]. The NSA and the Navy purchased some 450 TSEC/HW-8 delay compensators to be used together with KW-26 receivers [1].

The result was a combination of military, political, and strategic impacts. Military-wise, it offered protection for SIGINT and other record traffic for all services, including ships at sea [1]. In terms of politics, it provided protection for diplomatic traffic in State Department circuits. Strategically, it provided NSA analysts with a secure connection to SIGINT collection sites during the Cold War period [1] and contributed to the establishment of the agency as the nation's cryptographic authority [2].

## Impact on Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability

*Confidentiality:* The most significant advantage of the KW-26 was confidentiality. The machine mixed each plaintext bit with a keystream bit using Vernam's modulo-2 addition (XOR) operation, and the receiver mixed the same keystream with the received message [1]. In addition, the device offered traffic flow security, which hid whether real messages were being transmitted. Such security was important since the amount of traffic itself might indicate military operations regardless of the secrecy of individual messages [1]. Key management was another source of security measures. Daily cards could be used only once since the fill device slit the card when the card was removed from the machine [1]. Moreover, the key generator created 32 times more key material than it actually used, using only one of every 32 key elements [2]. Thus, any machine failure or reuse of a key would leave too little traffic in depth for successful cryptanalysis [2]. Emission security was another goal of the design [1].

*Integrity:* Integrity was the KW-26's most vulnerable aspect. The sources mention no method for authentication or tamper detection, and transmission errors would just appear as scrambled text that needed to be retransmitted [1]. An operator could tell from the scrambled text that something was wrong, but nothing could verify that a message was unchanged or that it was sent by a genuine sender. The daily key card provided a very weak, implicit form of authentication, in that nobody without the card could generate new readable traffic. XOR encryption is also malleable; that is, flipping a bit in the ciphertext flips the corresponding bit in the plaintext, and there was nothing in the system that would notice. Section VIII returns to this gap.

*Availability:* Availability was one of the strengths in this device. It had magnetic cores which were highly reliable and consumed less energy than the vacuum tubes, and the entirely electronically made device had no moving parts to wear out [1], [2]. On ship, the KW-26 was able to withstand the shocks and vibrations caused by the operation of sixteen-inch guns, and it was highly reliable with very little downtime [1]. Since it could do on-line encryption, it never became a bottleneck; one NSA test engineer concluded that on-line electronic encryption enhanced the system's performance [1]. When changes in path delay broke synchronization, the TSEC/HW-8 add-on restored it without a full restart about 90 percent of the time [1].

# General Characteristics

The KW-26 was made up of two separate units, the transmitter and receiver, that supported 100 words per minute tele-typewriter communication circuits [1]. In other words, the KW-26 was an on-line machine. This means that the messages were encrypted as they were being sent. Also, it was a synchronous machine, meaning the transmitter and receiver had to produce the exact same keystream, bit by bit and in lockstep. Every day a new punched key card was locked to the machine's Common Fill Device that was responsible for reading 45 columns of data and setting the cryptographic parameters [1], [2]. The cryptographic parameters established the keystream generator using Fibonacci shift registers, but in the case of NSA's initial breadboard machine a technique named "Koken algorithm" was used to establish the keystream generator [1], [2]. Keystream was XOR-ed with plaintext and at the receiver end the operation was inverted as mentioned in Section III [1]. Since the cryptographic process took place electronically, the maximum speed was dictated only by the teletypewriter attached to the machine [2].

Not relays and rotors but BIMAG (Bi-Stable Magnetic Element) cores served as the basis of the logical elements of the KW-26; in addition, BIMAG cores made up its shift registers, and each unit had more than 800 cores and about 50 vacuum-tube drivers [1]. BIMAG cores were assembled into small modules mounted onto the several-layered boards; there were five boards in each transmitter or receiver [1]. Each unit took slightly more than half of a standard relay rack and consumed about 1 kW of power, mainly used by vacuum tubes for interface to various inputs and outputs [1].

# Usage Domains

The KW-26 was used by almost every agency in the U.S. national security community, and its users selected the equipment for a common set of reasons. The device provided security for traffic at all classification levels, relieved users of the hassle of one-time tape, offered traffic flow security, and operated directly over commercial communication circuits [1]. There were additional reasons, unique to each agency, that the KW-26 was selected. The NSA placed the highest priority on traffic flow security for the CRITICOMM connections between SIGINT sites and analysts [1], because the flow of intelligence traffic itself could reveal what the United States was gathering and where. The Navy required record communications that would continue to operate under the shock and vibration experienced on board ships [1]. The DCA and the Army and Air Force operated backbone, tactical, torn-tape, and store-and-forward message networks, where an off-line encryption process would have slowed down the traffic flow [1]. The State Department and CIA needed secure point-to-point circuits for diplomatic and intelligence communications [1].

Daily use of the KW-26 was carried out by communication-center operators who set up the key cards for the day and kept their circuits synchronized [1]. This system was in itself a major development. Cryptographic equipment had previously been placed in its own separate, vault-like facility called a cryptocenter, but the KW-26 was the first major cryptographic equipment intended for rack mounting within the communication center where the teletypes were located [2]. This meant that encryption ceased being a specialized process performed in another location and became an ordinary process in message handling. A secondary group of users was NSA's telecommunications organization (TCOM), whose engineers tested and evaluated the equipment [1].

# Successors and Descendants

The KW-26 demonstrated that an electronic key generator could take the place of one-time tape. However, it was a point-to-point system, meaning that there had to be one machine for every link [2]. This requirement immediately led to the next important generation. The Navy had to broadcast simultaneously to hundreds of ships, let any ship come in late and start receiving with only that day's key card, and do so without a return path [2]. NSA's solution was the KW-37. Its transmitter started each day at a certain time – 0800 Greenwich time – and any receiver that came in late started its generator with the same initial condition and ran it at 570 times the usual speed until it caught up, which took between several seconds and two minutes [2]. As one bad transmitter could send gibberish to the whole fleet, the KW-37 used three key generators and trusted only the output that was agreed on by at least two of them [2]. The early experiments showed the weakness of this approach – two generators missing the same circuit boards agreed and overruled the only correct generator. This problem was solved by interlocks [2].

From the mid-1980s on, the KW-26 was replaced by the more modern solid-state data encryptor TSEC/KG-84 [1]. In a wider context, the basic concept which the KW-26 demonstrated on a large scale - a shift register keystream generator operated by a small daily key - became a fundamental component of later stream ciphers. No KW-26s remain in use anymore; the machine is fully retired, and the first KW-26s have been archived at the NSA [1].

# Retirement

NSA initiated the decommissioning of the KW-26 in the mid-1980s, and it took several years for that to happen [1]. After its first deliveries in 1957, the device was in use for more than 25 years through most of the Cold War [1]. Its decommissioning was mostly due to aging rather than any kind of malfunction. The KW-26 required 1950s vacuum tubes and magnetic cores, occupied slightly more than half of a relay rack, and consumed about 1 kW [1], which was an expensive amount considering the solid-state hardware available by the 1980s. Besides, its point-to-point nature made it inefficient on a large scale like the installation of 336 units simultaneously in Fort Meade [2]. Finally, its successor, KG-84, was a data encryptor [1], which better suited communication systems which were moving beyond teletypewriter traffic.

The process of retiring itself was a process of ensuring security. Those parts which would have been capable of exposing any clues regarding the cryptography were returned either to the NSA Information Assurance Directorate or some other facility for the purpose of securely destroying them [1]. Other parts of the unit, like the aluminum case, the power supply, and the input/output components, had their labels stripped off and were melted down [1]. Thus the KW-26 got extra protection of confidentiality well after the life of the machine. The first units, as mentioned in Section VI, were put away in the NSA archives [1].

# Security Limitation and Solution

The biggest problem with the KW-26 encryption was that the system offered confidentiality without guaranteeing integrity or authenticity [1]. Due to XOR function, which links a single bit of the cipher text with a single bit of the keystream, changing a bit in the former will change the same bit in the deciphered text. If an attacker were familiar with the structure of the message, i.e., where the time, or the quantity in question is positioned, he could change this information without actually knowing anything about the key itself, while the receiver would receive a seemingly normal text. This kind of attack was a realistic threat due to the fact that the KW-26 was used over commercially operated communications lines [1] (in other words, on the infrastructure which was not controlled by the government). In addition, there was no way for the receiver to realize this: the only sign of something going wrong was garbled text [1], and a carefully modified message would not look garbled. NSA did develop a remedy, the HW-8, for a different issue described in Section III, the loss of synchronization, but the sources describe no fix for this one.

The simple solution is to include a message authentication code (MAC), computed using an additional key. The sender computes a short tag using the message and the key and sends it along with the message; the recipient recomputes the tag and any modification to the message, even a single bit, results in a mismatch. So a MAC provides message integrity and also authenticates the sender, who must have possessed the key. This is actual authentication compared to mere protection provided by the daily key card. Modern systems accomplish this by combining encryption and authentication in one step through authenticated encryption algorithms like AES-GCM. In favor of the KW-26 designers, computation of a cryptographic MAC in real time would have been extremely challenging using 800 cores and less than 5 kHz clock [1].

# Conclusion

TSEC/KW-26 demonstrated that an electronic key generator could be used in place of one-time tape for almost all of the high-level circuits, and through more than 25 years of use, it protected traffic for virtually all aspects of the U.S. national security community [1], [2]. Its strengths were confidentiality and availability. Keystream encryption, traffic flow security, and one-time key cards ensured the security of message content, and reliable, robust, on-line equipment assured circuit availability, thus earning NSA a reputation as the nation's cryptographic authority [2]. Its weakness was integrity. Because there was no way to verify if the message had been tampered with, the KW-26 system effectively assumed that any message decrypting successfully was authentic. The program also offered a lesson in systems engineering. As Klein writes, without a central authority controlling the requirements, users constantly demanded additional modes and transmission speeds, most of which were never actually used, and thus the additional effort slowed down deliveries [1]. Engineers still fight the same battles today, and they now design systems from the ground up using authenticated encryption so that any message proves its authenticity along with keeping its contents confidential.
